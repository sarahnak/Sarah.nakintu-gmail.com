FieldEval

FieldEval tests whether AI safeguards hold in the languages people actually use. Trained native speakers test the chatbots people rely on, findings go privately to the labs that can fix them, and we retest.

Download the one-pager (PDF) →

Why African languages

Most AI safety training and testing happens in English, and recent research shows safeguards don't reliably carry over to African languages.

  • Dr Seid Muhie Yimam and colleagues (2026) found harmful prompts in Twi, Hausa, Amharic and Swahili keep less than 10% of the English refusal signal in most models tested.
  • Marx and Dunaiski (2026) found that in Afrikaans, Kiswahili, isiXhosa and isiZulu, attacks over several messages still work where single messages fail, and human red-teamers succeed more often than automated methods.
  • TukaBench (2026) found models often give confused answers instead of refusing, so a low attack rate doesn't mean a model is safe.

No published study has tested whether chatbots refuse harmful requests from fluent Luganda or Lusoga speakers. That's the gap FieldEval starts with.

How it works

  • University students who are native speakers are trained as safety testers.
  • They test the chatbots people use most, in single messages and longer conversations, using lower-harm stand-in categories, never weapons.
  • Findings go privately to the affected labs first. A public summary, with no working attacks, follows after a fix or after 90 days.
  • We retest to see whether the fix holds.

Where we're starting

The first pilot runs with Musizi University in Kampala, testing Luganda and Lusoga, from December 2026 to June 2027. See the Musizi pilot →

Where it could go

The same approach could check AI tools people already rely on, like farm advice apps, and support government testing teams in other African countries. If the pilot works, the plan is a network of African university testing teams.

My role

I lead the partnerships, programs and funding: getting labs, testers and a university to agree on access, data handling and disclosure. Technical methods are owned by university faculty and our research adviser.
